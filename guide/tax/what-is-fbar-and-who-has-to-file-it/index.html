What is FBAR in US tax law?

FBAR stands for Foreign Bank Account Report, a form required by the US Treasury Department to report foreign financial accounts. It is filed electronically using FinCEN Form 114 and is separate from federal tax returns. The purpose of FBAR is to help the US government track financial assets held abroad to combat tax evasion and money laundering. Accounts that must be reported include bank accounts, brokerage accounts, mutual funds, and certain types of insurance policies. The reporting threshold is $10,000 in aggregate across all foreign accounts at any time during the calendar year. FBAR filings are due annually by April 15, with an automatic extension to October 15.

Who is required to file FBAR in the US?

FBAR must be filed by US persons, including citizens, residents, and entities like corporations or partnerships, with foreign financial accounts exceeding $10,000 in aggregate value at any point during the year. This includes individuals living abroad and dual citizens. Entities created or organized under US law are also required to file. Non-residents who meet the criteria must file as well. Failure to file can result in penalties ranging from $10,000 for non-willful violations to $100,000 or 50% of the account balance for willful violations. The IRS estimates that over 1 million FBARs are filed annually.

What types of accounts must be reported on FBAR?

FBAR requires reporting of foreign financial accounts, including bank accounts, savings accounts, checking accounts, securities accounts, brokerage accounts, mutual funds, and certain insurance policies with cash value. Accounts held in foreign branches of US banks are also reportable if they meet the $10,000 threshold. Joint accounts, accounts with signature authority, and accounts owned by entities where the filer has a controlling interest must also be reported. Cryptocurrency accounts are generally not reportable on FBAR unless held in a foreign financial account. The aggregate value of all accounts determines the filing requirement.

What is the deadline for filing FBAR?

The FBAR filing deadline is April 15 each year, aligning with the federal tax return due date. However, an automatic extension is granted until October 15, with no need to file a separate extension request. Late filings may incur penalties unless the filer can demonstrate reasonable cause. For 2023, the IRS processed over 1.2 million FBARs, with a significant portion filed during the extended period. Filers who miss the deadline but have no tax liability or unpaid taxes may qualify for the Streamlined Filing Compliance Procedures, which reduce or eliminate penalties.

What are the penalties for failing to file FBAR?

Penalties for failing to file FBAR depend on whether the violation is willful or non-willful. Non-willful violations can result in a penalty of up to $10,000 per account per year. Willful violations can lead to penalties of $100,000 or 50% of the account balance at the time of the violation, whichever is greater. Criminal penalties, including fines and imprisonment, may apply in severe cases. The IRS collected over $100 million in FBAR penalties in 2022. Filers who voluntarily disclose unreported accounts may qualify for reduced penalties under the Offshore Voluntary Disclosure Program.

How do I calculate the $10,000 FBAR threshold?

The $10,000 FBAR threshold is calculated by aggregating the maximum value of all foreign financial accounts throughout the calendar year. For example, if you have three accounts with maximum balances of $4,000, $3,500, and $3,000, the total is $10,500, triggering the filing requirement. The value must be converted to US dollars using the Treasury’s Financial Management Service exchange rate on the last day of the year. Accounts with zero balance or those closed during the year must still be included if they exceeded the threshold at any point. The IRS provides detailed guidance on calculating account values.

Does FBAR apply to US citizens living abroad?

Yes, FBAR applies to US citizens living abroad if they meet the $10,000 threshold in foreign accounts. This includes expatriates, dual citizens, and green card holders. Even if taxes are paid in the country of residence, FBAR filing is still required. The IRS reported that in 2022, over 300,000 FBARs were filed by US citizens living overseas. Failure to file can result in penalties, regardless of residency status. Some countries have tax treaties with the US, but these do not exempt individuals from FBAR requirements. Expats should ensure compliance to avoid fines.

How does FBAR differ from Form 8938?

FBAR and Form 8938 both report foreign financial assets but differ in scope and purpose. FBAR, filed with FinCEN, applies to accounts exceeding $10,000 in aggregate value and includes bank accounts, brokerage accounts, and mutual funds. Form 8938, filed with the IRS, applies to assets exceeding $50,000 ($100,000 for married couples) and includes additional assets like foreign pensions and interests in foreign entities. In 2022, over 1 million FBARs were filed, compared to 800,000 Form 8938s. Both forms may be required for the same taxpayer, depending on their financial situation.
